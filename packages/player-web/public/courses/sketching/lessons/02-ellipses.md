# Ellipses

A circle seen from an angle is an ellipse. Since you almost never look at anything dead-on, you will draw far more ellipses than circles.

## Three things an ellipse is not

:::widget type="multiple-choice" id="ellipses-shape"
prompt: Which shape is a correctly drawn ellipse?
options:
  - text: A rectangle with rounded corners
    explanation: That is a rounded rectangle — the sides are straight. An ellipse has no straight part.
  - text: Two arcs meeting at points, like an eye
    explanation: That is a lens (vesica). An ellipse has no corners.
  - text: A smooth, symmetric oval with no corners or straight sides
    correct: true
hint: 'Picture the rim of a glass seen from a little above. What does its outline look like at the far left and right?'
explanation: 'An ellipse is a circle seen at an angle, so it keeps the circle''s smoothness — no corners and no straight parts — and it is symmetric across both its long and short axes. The ends are rounded, and the most common mistake is drawing them as points, like an eye or a lemon.'
:::

## Exercise 3 — rounds and rounds

The trick is the same as for lines: ghost the motion above the page, several times, then lower the pen while the arm is already moving. Go round **two or three times** in one continuous stroke.

:::widget type="sketch" id="ellipses-trace"
prompt: |
  Trace each of the three ellipses, going round two or three times without lifting the pen. The left one is narrow, the right one almost a circle.
trace: assets/images/trace-ellipses.png
widths: [3]
minStrokes: 3
rubric:
  - Each ellipse is one continuous stroke
  - The loops overlap closely instead of spiralling away
  - There are no corners at the ends
explanation: 'Going round two or three times without lifting smooths out the shape. Your arm settles into an even, elliptical motion, and the loops average out the wobble. Pointed ends usually come from slowing down at the turn, so keep the speed constant all the way round.'
:::

:::widget type="sketch" id="ellipses-free"
prompt: |
  Without a trace: draw a row of five ellipses getting progressively **wider**, from a thin sliver on the left to a near-circle on the right. This is what a coin does as you tilt it towards you.
widths: [3]
minStrokes: 5
rubric:
  - The five ellipses share a horizontal centre line
  - The width grows steadily from left to right
explanation: 'This is what makes ellipses useful: the width tells the viewer the angle. A thin sliver means you are looking at the circle edge-on; a near-circle means you are looking straight down at it. Get the widths right and a row of coins or the rims of a cylinder look tilted correctly without any other help.'
:::
